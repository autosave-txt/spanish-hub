Verb Deep-Dive: haber

Not "to have (possess)" — that's tener. Haber has two jobs: the impersonal "hay" (there is/are), and the auxiliary that builds every compound tense.
haber is one of the strangest verbs in Spanish because you almost never use it to mean "I have a thing" — that's tener's job (see the tener page). Instead haber does two very different jobs: as hay, it means "there is / there are" and never changes for person or number. As an auxiliary (he, has, ha, hemos, habéis, han), it's the verb that builds every compound tense — "I have eaten" is he comido, built from haber + a past participle, exactly like English "have" + "-ed".

hay — there is / there are

The single most useful form of haber — and the one you'll use constantly from day one

Unlike every other Spanish verb, hay never conjugates for person or number — it's the same word whether you mean "there is one chair" or "there are twenty chairs." It comes from haber but functions completely on its own.

Examples
Hay un problema.
There is a problem.
Hay tres personas en la sala.
There are three people in the room.
No hay pan.
There isn't any bread.
¿Hay un baño cerca?
Is there a bathroom nearby?
📝 Common trap: English speakers reach for está/están (from estar) when they mean "there is/are" — but estar is for locating something specific you already know exists ("the keys are on the table" = las llaves están en la mesa), while hay introduces something for the first time ("there are keys somewhere" = hay llaves).

📋 Quick reference — auxiliary forms side by side

hay que + infinitivo

Impersonal obligation — "one must / it's necessary to," with no specific subject

This uses the impersonal hay the same way as "there is," but paired with que + an infinitive it means a general obligation that applies to everyone, not one specific person — closer to "you have to" as a general rule than "I have to" (that's tener que — see the tener page).

Examples
Hay que estudiar mucho.
You have to study a lot. (in general)
Hay que ser paciente.
One has to be patient.
No hay que preocuparse.
There's no need to worry.
🔗 See also: for obligation aimed at a specific person ("I have to work," "you have to call her"), use tener que instead — covered on the tener page.
